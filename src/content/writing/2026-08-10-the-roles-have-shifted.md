---
title: The Roles Have Shifted
date: 2026-08-10
excerpt: And your role is no longer you
---
About 6 months ago, our company made the conscious decision to give ourselves the time and space to experiment with a new operating model. One that was built on a few bets and beliefs. Among those was the hypothesis that with the advance of AI, just about anyone will be able to build anything, eventually. We took two weeks, which turned into four weeks, which turned into...well, the present...to try it out. Here's how it looked at the start:

You have an idea. You enter a repo. Your idea is automatically validated or invalidated against KPIs based on the discussion and evidence surrounding the idea. You build it out locally with guidance. You commit. It's reviewed. It ships. It gets measured. It stays or is killed.

Sounds easy enough. But the challenge was, *anyone* can do it. Our sales, marketing, product, design, engineering folks...you name any role and they had the opportunity to build and ship. As you can imagine, there was a fuck ton of guardrails put into place, which I had a major hand in. But even those are beginning to loosen tremendously in time.

We called this user/persona: the builder. 

Over the course of the 6 months since its inception, we've found ourselves organically falling into "roles" that are less articulated, and more enveloping. More orchestral in nature. We're not individually working because we're 'here to do a specific thing' any longer. Hell, I joined Workweek as a Founding Product Designer because of my craft and my deep knowledge in Figma and design systems. That knowledge has proven to be highly valuable, but the actual tactical work? Nothing like what I was doing last year. As my CEO tweeted out recently, I haven't used Figma to do really anything in over 2 months. 

And that's the way forward. Here's how us humans have sifted out into the model. There are the following:

Builders: Those with an idea who build them out tangibly to the degree they're killed or shipped. They connect dots innovate and/or iterate on existing or new features or product ideas while ensuring they're serving a business goal both before and after hitting production.

Machinists: Those who are deeply engaged with maintaining, scaling and evolving the model by which we work. They are the ones who are continuously building to enhance the very experience of building while ensuring low risk to the builders and the product(s) themselves.

Polishers: Those who are keenly involved in ensuring what's being shipped has as few rough edges and fail points as possible. Not to be dismissed as simply "cleaning up someone else's work," they're truly the ones who work to capture the essence of the idea through the moments and touchpoints that make or break a user's impression and relationship with the product.

The best part is any one individual is very likely in more than one of these camps. I've found myself in all three even at one time. This is not to say I'm working more or doing multiple jobs. That would be comparing what I'm doing to a legacy format of work motivated by role definition. 

I'm just *making* things work and *making* things happen. And sometimes, they don't work out! But most of the time, that's entirely okay. It's a faster, more robust, more contextually saturated way of operating, but it's where I (we) find ourselves today.

There's a lot of sauce behind the scenes that I won't cover in this single entry. But all this is to say, thinking about my work as "designing" and my role as a "designer," I believe, is fast becoming antiquated. 

I'm just lucky enough to be someone who can sit with a swirl of ambiguity around the byline of their name and not freak the fuck out :)
