title: "Doing What's Hard On Purpose"
date: 2026-07-28

Like so many others, I went to see Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey recently. I loved it, but mostly because I love monster movies and I believe this was actually a monster movie. The thing with monster movies is they're perfect cases for pushing technology to achieve look, feel, expression that is often unrealistic. So one might think, this was the perfect opportunity to leverage AI.

Of course, Nolan didn't. (I mean he probably did, but didn't rely entirely on it)

His work is a case study in intentional constraint. He leans on practical effects, camera tricks, physical sets, etc. not because he has to but because those limitations are, I bet he'd say, what shape the creative outcome. His decision to not simulate everything becomes a statement about taste and authorship — and about what "real" feels like on a screen now.

At Workweek – the company I am a part of – we cherish and empower professionals who live real workplace and industry experiences, and their voices that those experiences have shaped. We believe the raw creator is of great value. And people clearly agree based on our communities' platforms. 

Filmmakers like Nolan who publicly commit to physical craft, real performances, and clearly human decision-making become more valuable, not less. The news, sales, and seats are proving it. People will pay to see something they know was actually created – visioned – by a human: actors genuinely acting, a miniature genuinely exploding, a 70mm frame that captures actual light bouncing off actual objects.

The Odyssey is enormous in so many ways. But most of all it's an incredibly distinct and powerful proposal that we don't absolutely need AI to achieve what we humans consider greatness (yet). If you strip filmmaking down to simply "convincing moving images that hold attention," then sure, AI will end up taking the cake. But if you see filmmaking as "a particular human mind wrestling with reality, using images and sound," then Nolan is an argument that this kind of authorship still matters. Probably the most profound argument of this year, oddly enough.
